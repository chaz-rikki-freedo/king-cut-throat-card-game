# Characters

A background for each of the ten opponents. This page is reference material only. The game doesn't show these stories.

## Vex

Vex was born in the back seat of a moving Buick somewhere between two towns, and the birth certificate lists the county as "unknown, possibly two." Vex's family ran a traveling knife-sharpening business out of a converted bread van, and Vex spent their childhood in motel parking lots, learning long division from a library book that was eleven years overdue. At fourteen, Vex won a regional spelling bee under a name that wasn't theirs and has never explained why.

As an adult, Vex has been a ferry purser, a hotel night auditor, a professional mourner for hire for one winter, and briefly the voice on a regional airline's hold music announcements ("Your call is important to us" is still Vex, if you call at the right hour). Vex now owns a narrow bar near the ferry terminal called The Second Crossing, which has no sign, no menu and a jukebox that plays only waltzes. Vex irons their shirts every morning, even on days they don't leave the apartment, and keeps a drawer of ferry tickets from crossings they never took. Vex is allergic to cats, owns two anyway, and has named both of them Gerald.

Vex's only known relative is a great-uncle who farms turnips three counties over and writes Vex long, painfully honest letters about soil, weather and their own feelings. Vex, who has never told anyone a true thing about themselves, reads every letter aloud to both Geralds and keeps them in a shoebox marked "TAXES." The one public fact about Vex is that they were once crowned King of the Harbor Festival, against their will, when the parade float carrying the real King broke down outside The Second Crossing and the crown had to go on the nearest head. There is a photograph. Vex has bought every copy they could find.

**At the table:** Vex leads trump, wins big and cashes Kings early, and feints more than anyone. Vex half-believes what others show.

## Doc

Doc was the only doctor in Wren Valley for thirty-one years. They delivered three hundred and six babies, eleven calves (in emergencies), and one llama, which the town still talks about at the harvest fair. Doc's truck had two hundred and ninety thousand miles on it when it finally stopped, in the middle of a covered bridge, and Doc walked the last mile to a house call carrying their bag and a jar of the patient's mother's pickles.

Retirement has not suited Doc. They reorganized the town library by the Dewey decimal system without being asked, then reorganized it again when they discovered a mistake in their own work. Doc keeps bees, badly, and has been stung so often that they now consider it a form of conversation. Every Sunday, Doc writes a letter to a pen pal in Finland whom they have never met, and has done so for forty-two years. Neither of them has ever suggested a phone call. Doc's great secret, known only to the pharmacist, is that they faint at the sight of their own blood but no one else's.

Doc's Finnish pen pal turned out, around letter three hundred, to be a reindeer farmer, and the two now trade advice on difficult births, stubborn livestock and stubborn people. The valley's farmers voted Doc Harvest King three years running, mostly because nobody else would wear the crown, which is woven from corn husks and makes everyone sneeze, including Doc. The town's most trusted healer spent each coronation with a streaming nose, prescribing antihistamines to the crowd from the parade stage.

**At the table:** Doc wins with the cheapest card and holds Kings late. Doc rarely feints, and stops believing a player after one caught bluff.

## Mei

Mei is an actuary at a mid-sized insurance firm, where they calculate how likely it is that a person will slip on a wet floor in a supermarket in the next fiscal year. Mei finds this fascinating and will explain it to you at length if you let them. Mei grew up above their grandparents' laundromat, where they learned to fold a fitted sheet into a perfect square by the age of six and to read the dryers' sounds the way other children read clocks.

Mei has kept a daily log since they were nine. Every entry has the date, the weather, what they ate, how long the train was late, and one sentence about something a stranger said. There are now thirty-one volumes. Mei once used entry 4,112 to prove to a landlord that a leak had begun in March, not June, and won back a full deposit. Mei runs one marathon a year, always the same one, and has improved their time by exactly four seconds every year for nine years. They have no idea how this keeps happening and find it slightly unnerving. Mei's guilty pleasure is reality television about people renovating houses badly.

The irony isn't lost on Mei: the actuary who prices the risk of slipping in a supermarket has slipped in a supermarket four times. All four are logged, with the store, the aisle and the brand of floor cleaner. Every autumn, Mei's grandparents' laundromat holds a folding showdown against the dry cleaner across the street, and Mei has defended the family title for eleven straight years. The trophy is a gold-painted iron, and the local paper calls Mei the Fitted Sheet King, a title Mei corrects in a letter to the editor every year.

**At the table:** Mei has no habits and never feints: Mei counts, and reads what the table shows with a cool head.

## Ace

Ace manages the Elm Street Community Center, a converted fire station that still has the brass pole, which Ace slides down every morning to unlock the front doors. Ace once played three seasons as a minor-league shortstop for a team called the Riverton Mudcats, batting .241 and stealing exactly one base in their whole career. The base is mounted on the office wall with a small brass plaque.

Ace organizes everything: the bake sale, the summer softball league, the seniors' tango night, the annual chili cook-off that has been won by the same retired mail carrier for nineteen years. Ace has never refused a dare and has the injuries to prove it, including a scar from a bet involving a unicycle and a parking garage ramp. Ace calls everyone "champ" because they forget names under pressure, which nobody has figured out in fifteen years. At home, Ace lives alone with a tank of very old goldfish, one of which may be older than the community center's boiler. Ace reads poetry before bed and would rather die than admit it.

The retired mail carrier who has won the chili cook-off nineteen years running calls themselves the Chili King and wears a paper crown to the judging. Ace has entered every year, lost every year, and treats the rivalry as the great showdown of their life. The irony is that Ace is allergic to chili peppers. Ace has never tasted their own entry, nor anyone else's, and relies on a panel of volunteer tasters, all of whom secretly vote for the Chili King.

**At the table:** Ace leads Kings and plays them early, bluffs now and then, and trusts what others show.

## Viv

Viv toured for eleven years as a concert pianist and saw most of the world's great cities from hotel windows at four in the morning. A wrist injury ended the career in the middle of a Rachmaninoff concerto, and Viv finished the piece with one hand. The review called it "an unusual interpretation." Viv framed it.

Viv now teaches five students, never six, from a third-floor apartment crowded with houseplants, each of which is named after a composer who died in poverty. Viv drinks Earl Grey with no sugar from the same chipped cup every day, wears gloves from October to April, and has strong opinions about fonts. They can't drive and have never needed to. Every Tuesday, Viv buys one lemon from the same fruit stand, though they've never been seen using a lemon for anything. Viv's students say a compliment from Viv comes about once a year and is worth the wait. Viv has a younger sibling who sells insurance, and they talk on the phone every Sunday about absolutely nothing for two hours.

The lemons come from a stall at the Tuesday farmers' market, run by a citrus grower who was once Viv's great rival at the conservatory. Twenty years ago, the two met in a famous piano showdown in Vienna that ended in a draw when the lid of the second piano fell shut. The rival quit music the next day to grow lemons. Neither has mentioned Vienna since. Every Tuesday, Viv buys one lemon, the grower says "Good choice," and Viv says "I know." It's the longest conversation either of them has with anyone all week.

**At the table:** Viv leads long suits and holds back, waiting for the right trick, Kings saved for late. Viv feints often and catches feints quickly.

## Gus

Gus drives a refrigerated delivery truck full of frozen peas, fish sticks and, on alternate Thursdays, an unexplained crate of novelty ice. Gus has three dogs (Biscuit, Meatball and Professor), two children who are both taller than Gus now, and a backyard grill the size of a small car. Every Saturday from May to October, the whole block eats at Gus's place whether they planned to or not.

Gus has never been on time to anything, including their own wedding, which they missed the first half of because they stopped to help a stranger change a tire. The stranger is now godparent to both children. Gus once won a regional hot-dog-eating contest by accident: they were there to deliver the buns and got pulled in when a contestant fell ill. Gus collects bottle caps from every state and keeps them in a jar labeled "RETIREMENT." Gus's laugh has set off a car alarm at least twice. Gus cries at every movie, including documentaries about concrete.

Here's the irony: Gus, who feeds the whole block every weekend, lost their sense of smell in a childhood accident with a garden hose and can barely taste a thing. Gus cooks entirely by the sound of the sizzle and the faces of the people eating. Every Labor Day, Gus holds a grill showdown with a retired dairy farmer from across the street who is convinced Gus is cheating somehow. Biscuit, the oldest dog, was once crowned King of the Block at the neighborhood dog show, and Gus still introduces Biscuit to strangers by that title.

**At the table:** Gus wins big and trumps in whenever possible, believes almost anything, and feints mostly by accident.

## Zen

Zen spent eight years as a bond trader and was very good at it, until a Tuesday morning when they stood up in the middle of a trade, walked out of the building, and kept walking. They ended up at a bus station, bought a ticket to the farthest place on the board, and spent the next two years traveling with one backpack and one pair of socks they washed in hotel sinks.

Zen now runs a small yoga studio above a secondhand bookshop. The studio has creaky floors, a radiator that clanks in a rhythm Zen insists is meditative, and a cat that belongs to the bookshop but prefers the studio. Zen grows tomatoes on the roof, makes bread on Sundays, and answers most questions with a long pause followed by a short answer that's usually correct. Zen still dreams about the trading floor once a month and wakes up laughing. Old coworkers who visit the studio don't recognize Zen at first, and some of them have stayed for the class. Zen's one remaining vice is the stock market crossword in the Sunday paper, which they do in pen in under four minutes.

On the trading floor, colleagues called Zen the Ice King, for a temper nobody had ever seen and a stare that could stop a deal. During the backpack years, Zen worked eight months on a goat farm in exchange for a cot and two meals a day. The farmer, who didn't speak a word of Zen's language, taught them everything they now teach in the studio, mostly by pointing at goats. The studio sits directly above the bookshop's self-help section. Zen considers it the shop's busiest section and finds that very funny.

**At the table:** Zen leads long suits and wins cheap. Zen feints a little and forgives a feint easily.

## Lou

Lou works in the accounts office of Pemberton's Hardware, where they've reconciled the same receipt books for twenty-three years. Lou has worried about something every single day of their life. They check the stove three times before bed, carry an umbrella in a drought, and once apologized to a mannequin in a department store and then to the salesperson for apologizing to the mannequin.

Lou's cousin Bernadette has made it her mission to get Lou out of the house, and over the years she has dragged Lou to salsa lessons, a hot-air balloon festival, a silent retreat (Lou spoke twice, both times to apologize), and a polar bear plunge, which Lou now attends every New Year's Day and secretly loves. Lou keeps a list of "things that went better than expected" taped inside a kitchen cabinet. It has four hundred and twelve entries. Lou has a pet tortoise named Pace who is fifty-one years old and inherited from a great-aunt, and Lou worries constantly that Pace is bored. Pace is not bored.

Three autumns ago, Lou was crowned Pumpkin King at the county fair for a nine-hundred-pound pumpkin they hadn't meant to grow. It sprouted from a compost heap Lou had been too nervous to turn. Lou spent the whole ceremony apologizing to the real farmers in the line-up, one of whom has since become Lou's closest friend and drives Lou to the polar bear plunge every New Year's Day. The crown sits on Pace the tortoise's shelf, and Pace seems to approve.

**At the table:** Lou holds back, throws low and keeps Kings late. Lou never feints and believes everything.

## Kit

Kit moved to town six weeks ago for a first job at the public library, shelving returns and running the Tuesday toddler story hour, which terrifies them. Kit grew up on a small island with one school, one shop and a ferry twice a day, and this is the first time they've lived somewhere with traffic lights. Kit still stops at every green one, just in case.

Kit keeps a notebook of local words and phrases they don't understand yet ("hella," "the Loop," "a mandatory fun event") and looks one up every night. Kit reads three books a week, all from the returns cart, in whatever order they come. Kit owns one plant, a cactus named Sir Reginald, which they over-water out of affection. Kit says yes to every invitation, even the frightening ones, because they promised their grandmother they would. So far that has meant a karaoke night, a bowling league, a stranger's wedding, and a book club where Kit was the only member who had read the book. Kit called home after each one.

Kit's grandmother is a kelp farmer on the island and the reigning champion of its oldest tradition: the midsummer shearing showdown, where islanders race to shear a sheep before the ferry docks. Kit has lost every year since the age of eight. At the library, the toddlers have crowned Kit the King of the Rug, with a cardboard crown that Kit wears to every story hour. The irony is that the first book they asked Kit to read was about a King with no clothes, and Kit, terrified of reading aloud, was the one who wanted to hide.

**At the table:** Kit leads Kings and plays them early, never feints, and takes every play at face value.

## Tex

Tex grew up on a cattle ranch with more cows than neighbors and learned to rope before they learned to read. Since leaving home, Tex has been a rodeo clown, a long-haul trucker, a short-order cook, a lighthouse keeper for one very long winter, and the second-best accordion player at a wedding band that had only two accordion players. Tex quit every one of those jobs on a whim, usually on a Friday, and usually with a good story.

Tex owns one hat, one guitar, a harmonica that plays only in E, and a pickup truck named Delores that has been in four states without valid plates. Tex has a scar for every year of their life and will tell you the story of any of them, though the stories change. Tex has never planned a day, never used a calendar and once arrived at a friend's birthday party a full year early, then stayed for the party the next year too. Tex sends their mother a postcard from every town they pass through. She has more than six hundred of them pinned to the kitchen wall in a map of everywhere Tex has been.

Tex has ridden bulls, wrestled steers and once talked down an escaped circus camel, but Tex is terrified of chickens and has been since the ranch. Tex's most famous showdown, at high noon in the town square of a place they no longer remember, turned out to be a pie-eating contest. Tex lost to a seventy-year-old peach farmer who has since sent Tex a pie every Christmas. Delores, the pickup truck, was bought from a man who swore he was the King of Rock and Roll in hiding. Tex believes him.

**At the table:** Tex leads trump, wins big and trumps in, and bluffs often and badly.
